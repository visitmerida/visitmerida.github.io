Cenotes Near Mérida: The Best Ones to Swim In

Yucatán's underground rivers surface in thousands of cenotes — and some of the most spectacular are within easy reach of Mérida. Here's where to swim, how to get there, and how to do it safely.

Cenote Ik Kil — the famous open sinkhole

A short drive from Chichén Itzá, Cenote Ik Kil is the kind of place that makes you understand why the Maya considered cenotes sacred. This open-air sinkhole drops about 26 meters down, its walls draped in vines and roots while a shaft of sunlight pierces the turquoise water below.

Swimming here is the main event — the water is cool, deep, and refreshing after the ruins. Life jackets are usually available for rent or included with entry, which makes it comfortable even for less confident swimmers. Changing rooms, lockers, and a restaurant on site make the visit easy. It pairs perfectly with a morning at Chichén Itzá, but it also works as a standalone half-day escape. Go early for the calmest water and the best light.

Cenote Xlacah — ruins and a swim in one trip

Want ruins and a swim in a single half-day? Head to Dzibilchaltún, only about 30 minutes east of Mérida. The archaeological site's centerpiece is the Temple of the Seven Dolls, named for the small clay figurines found inside and famous for the way the equinox sun aligns with its doorways. The site also holds one of the longest continuously occupied settlements in the Maya world, plus an on-site museum with artifacts found at the site.

Best of all, the open Cenote Xlacah sits right in the middle of the ruins — after walking the site in the heat, a dip in its clear water is pure bliss. Bring a swimsuit, towel, and water shoes, and come early to beat the midday sun.

The Homún cenote ring route

Southeast of Mérida, the village of Homún sits on the famous "Ring of Cenotes" — a circle of underground swimming holes left by the ancient meteor impact that shaped the peninsula. This is the place to swim in three or four cenotes in a single day.

Local mototaxi and tricycle drivers run the standard circuit: they'll take you from cenote to cenote, wait while you swim, and bring you back. Each cenote is different — open and sunlit, half-caved, or deep underground chambers reached by steep stairs. Go on a weekday to avoid crowds, and ask in the village square for the mototaxi circuit — everyone knows it.

Cenote safety: what to know before you swim

Yes, cenotes are generally safe to swim in, with some common sense. Stick to established cenotes that have staff, facilities, and other swimmers: these are managed, the water is monitored, and help is nearby.

  • Use a life jacket if you're not a strong swimmer — some cenotes are deep and the water is dark.
  • Don't dive. Depths vary, rocks hide below the surface, and diving is prohibited at many cenotes.
  • Shower before entering, as required, to protect the fragile ecosystem from sunscreen and oils.
  • Supervise children closely and skip the alcohol before swimming.
  • Wild, unmanaged cenotes carry real risks and are best left to experienced locals.

Getting to the cenotes without a tour

A cenote run is easy to organize without a tour. Ik Kil pairs naturally with the ruins trip — bus or taxi combos get you there. Xlacah is much closer to the city and reachable by bus plus a short taxi ride, making it a half-day option. Homún is about an hour southeast of Mérida by car or colectivo.

For multi-cenote days, a rental car is the real freedom play: pick three or four cenotes, pack a swimsuit and biodegradable sunscreen, and hop between them. Rinse off before swimming — sunscreen and insect repellent are banned in many cenotes — and bring cash for entries. For airport runs and late nights when the buses stop, ride apps are the easy option.
